The 4 stages of music production

What actually happens in each one, the test that closes it — and the order mistakes that turn a two-week track into a five-month one.

Ask ten producers what the stages of music production are and you'll get overlapping but fuzzy answers. The fuzziness is the problem: when the borders between stages are vague, work leaks between them — you mix while producing, rewrite while mixing — and every leak means doing something twice. Here's the clean version, with what belongs in each stage and the test that tells you it's over.

Stage 1 — Songwriting: the song as a written piece

Key and scale, chord progression, melody, and the section map (intro, verses, builds, drop or chorus, break, outro). No sound design yet, no perfect drums — a piano and a hummed melody are enough. This is where the emotional core is decided, and no later stage can add one that isn't there.

EXIT TEST: played on one instrument, the song still holds attention.

Stage 2 — Production: the song as a record

Sound selection and design, layering, drum programming, bass-and-kick relationship, transitions, fills, and the automation that keeps 3+ minutes alive. This is the longest stage and the one that swallows beginners — usually because they're secretly still songwriting (adding parts) or prematurely mixing (EQing sounds that will be replaced).

EXIT TEST: a full listen stays interesting with zero mixing moves; muting anything makes it worse.

Stage 3 — Mixing: the record on every speaker

Balance (volume first — it's the most powerful tool on the desk), EQ so elements stop fighting, compression with a named purpose, space front-to-back and left-to-right, and translation checks on real-world systems. Mixing is not "make everything better"; it's "make the arrangement's decisions audible everywhere".

EXIT TEST: loudness-matched against two references, it translates on earbuds, car and phone speaker. Full checklist: the mixing checklist.

Stage 4 — Mastering: the record ready for the world

Final EQ touches, competitive loudness without audible damage, and correct exports per platform. If the mix is right, mastering is short; mastering cannot rescue a broken mix, only certify a good one. Prepare the handoff properly: the mastering prep checklist.

EXIT TEST: loud enough next to your references, clean on the small speaker, exported and tagged.

The three order mistakes that cost months

  1. Mixing during production. Every sound you add after "mixing" invalidates the mix. Rough levels are fine; surgical EQ on a placeholder synth is time thrown away.
  2. Songwriting during mixing. "This verse needs rewriting" at the mix stage means two stages reopen at once. If the writing wasn't tested naked in stage 1, this is where it surfaces — at the most expensive possible moment.
  3. Mastering as repair. Loudness on top of a bad balance just makes the problems louder. When a master "doesn't glue", the defect is almost always two stages back.

The pattern behind all three: work leaking backwards. The fix is closing stages with an explicit test and not reopening them without naming a concrete defect — the method described in how to know when a song is finished.
