The mixing checklist

Every step in working order — because mixes don't fail from missing skill, they fail from steps done out of order or skipped under fatigue.

Two conditions before this list applies: the arrangement is final (mixing a moving target means mixing twice), and you have two reference tracks chosen — commercial songs in your genre whose mix you trust. Everything below assumes both.

1 · Session prep

Ten minutes here saves an hour of hunting later.

  • Tracks named and colored; buses created (drums, bass, music, vocals, FX)
  • Gain-staged: every fader near 0, no channel clipping into its first plugin
  • References imported into the session, level-matched to your rough mix
  • A break taken between finishing production and starting the mix — fresh ears

2 · Static balance

Volume is the most powerful tool on the desk. A great static balance is 70% of a great mix.

  • Full balance done with faders and pan only — zero plugins
  • The most important element (vocal or lead) set first; everything placed around it
  • Balance checked at low volume — priorities show up when it's quiet
  • Rough pan decisions made: what's center, what's wide

3 · EQ

EQ gives each element a home. If two sounds fight, one of them moves.

  • High-pass on everything that doesn't need lows (not the kick/bass)
  • Masking resolved between the pairs that always fight: kick–bass, vocal–synths, snare–claps
  • Cuts preferred over boosts where possible
  • Every EQ move A/B checked at matched loudness — louder always sounds "better"

4 · Compression

Compression needs a named purpose per track — control, glue or color. "Everyone compresses" is not a purpose.

  • Each compressor has a stated job you can say out loud
  • Gain reduction sane (typically 2-6 dB; more is a sound-design choice, made on purpose)
  • Bus compression on drums/mix bus subtle: 1-3 dB, slow enough to keep transients
  • Bypass-compared at matched loudness

5 · Space

Front-to-back with reverb and delay, left-to-right with pan. Depth is what makes a mix sound expensive.

  • Shared reverb/delay sends, not one insert per track
  • Something deliberately dry and up front — contrast is what reads as depth
  • Delay times in tempo; reverb tails not smearing the low end
  • Width checked: sides interesting, but the mix survives folded to mono

6 · Automation

A static mix is a photograph; a mixed song is a film.

  • Vocal/lead rides so every phrase stays intelligible
  • Section-to-section moves: choruses bigger, verses more intimate (1 dB is a lot)
  • FX automation on transitions (reverb throws, filter moves, delay tails)

7 · Translation — the exit test

The mix is done when it works everywhere, not when it's perfect somewhere.

  • Loudness-matched A/B against both references: no embarrassing gap in tone or punch
  • Checked on earbuds, phone speaker, car, and one loud full-range listen
  • Low end checked in mono
  • Each bus soloed once: no clicks, pops, clipped transients, bad fades
  • Headroom for mastering: peaks ~−3 to −6 dB, no limiter on the master bus
  • No defect you can name. "It doesn't feel right" after forty listens is fatigue — and fatigue is not information
